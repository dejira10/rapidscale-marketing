Why this case matters: it's where he states his **reply formula** most clearly, and shows how to handle a reply from someone who isn't the decision maker.

## 1. What he knew before sending

- **The market:**
  - Most daycares don't cook; they buy meals five days a week.
  - The US federal meal program for daycares (CACFP) is about $4B a year, about 1.7B meals.
  - Vendors pay for conference booths ($2,750–5,000), billboards and Google Ads to find daycares.
  - Daycares risk allergy and licensing problems with a bad vendor.
- **Data:** state licensing sites (newly licensed daycares, **with the opening date**), plus CACFP sponsor lists. He reruns it weekly: the "money loop."
- **Why not sell meetings:** "You're promising that another human being is going to show up … The meeting is not our product. The relationship is theirs."
- **Honesty level at send:** level 2.

## 2. The outreach (exact)

**To daycares (demand). Subject: "Lunch":**
> "Hey there, saw [Little Kids Castle] got its license on September 4th. Congrats. I work with food companies who deliver to daycares. Worth a look before the first month settles into a routine?"

**To food vendors (supply):**
> "I have daycares opening every week [licensed rooms with the seats counted and a kitchen that needs a delivery on Monday]. Before I send anyone anywhere, I just wanted to understand what you deliver and how far. Where do you deliver?"

## 3. The reply formula (his exact words)

> "Every reply we're going to send will basically have the same things … **One, say what they told you back to them. Two, don't hand them anything yet, meaning no names, no menus, no prices.** Whenever they ask these nuanced questions, I wouldn't answer them via email. I'll just get them on a quick call, because within the call you're going to get paid. **And always give them two times.**"

This is the call-based version. In the no-call cases (A01–A12) he sends the close email by email once both sides confirm. In this video the daycare side asked for menus and pricing, which are vendor details he didn't have yet.

## 5. Decision analysis

| Moment | What he did | Why (his words / **inference**) | Not done |
|---|---|---|---|
| A request for menus and pricing | A 10-minute call first, so he "only sends the ones that fit" | Holds the details back; gives a reason that serves them | Sending vendor names or menus |
| Mirroring | Repeated their own words ("still getting everything in place") | Step one of the formula | Generic replies |
| A non-decision-maker | Asked for the best person for a call | Licensing staff can't buy | Pitching the wrong person |
| Two times | Offered every time | Makes saying yes easy | "Let me know when you're free" |

## 6. Frankie comparison

- **"Say what they told you back"** is Frankie's parrot.
- **"Before I send menus and pricing, can we do a quick call so I only send you the ones that fit"** is Frankie's benevolent dictator: "Happy to, but first let's make sure we're a fit."
- **The difference:** Frankie would often send a Loom or Google Doc instead of a call. That's a design option for us when the details are ready.

## 7. Rules from this case

**Transferable:**

- The reply formula: mirror → hold details and names back → two times.
- A reason to hold details back that helps them: "so I only send you the ones that actually fit [their location]."
- A reply from a non-decision-maker: "Who's the best person on your team for a quick call?"
- License dates make timely, specific opening lines.
- Count only matches you can make today.

**Specific to this market:** CACFP, state childcare licensing, delivery radius.

**Do not copy:**

- "I'm speaking with a few daycares around DFW right now" was true only after daycares had replied. Fine at that point; not before.
- "I have daycares opening every week" was sent at level 2. Phrase it as "Daycares are opening every week in [area] (state license data)…"
