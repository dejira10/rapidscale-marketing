## 1. What he knew before sending

- **First-hand knowledge.** Saad owns a café, and knows owners "don't know which roaster to trust."
- **His market lens:** permanent demand. Coffee is "a consumable bought weekly by both sides forever," so every café is a live buyer and no trigger event is needed.
- **Offers he'd planned:**
  - **Café:** 2–3 roasters, with samples sent first, for $2,497.
  - **Roaster:** 3–6 cafés over 60–90 days, for $2,997–4,997.
- **Honesty level at send time:** level 2 on both sides. Every café buys beans; every roaster wants accounts.

## 2. The outreach (exact)

**To cafés (demand). Subject: "Beans":**
> "I work with wholesale coffee roasters actively taking on new café accounts right now. I'll have samples sent to your shop so you taste before you commit to anything."

**Café follow-up:**
> "Hey [name], are your regulars happy with the coffee, or are you still dialing in the beans?"

**To roasters (supply):**
> "I have cafés (opening cafés still dialing in their beans and operating shops that reorder every week) actively looking at their roaster relationships right now. Before I send anyone anywhere, I just wanted to understand your capacity and what you like to pour. Do you have capacity for new wholesale accounts right now?"

He deleted the social-proof line Claude added: "When you sell what people want, they don't really care about how you've done this before." He also chose the industry's own words ("wholesale accounts," "dialing in"). The follow-up is a discovery question about a common pain.

## 4. Charging both sides transparently (exact)

**In the café's close:**
> "For transparency, I'm also compensated independently by the roaster for originating and qualifying a live local account opportunity for them. This is a separate mandate on their side rather than part of Coco's fee."

**In the roaster's close:**
> "It's a newly opened independent coffee shop in [area]. I've spoken with the owner directly, and she specifically asked for a local roaster that could work with them on a house espresso / custom blend and eventually coffee under their own name. She's actively open to the relationship. My fee is $3K for identifying the live opportunity and establishing the right intent and requirements directly with the owner. For transparency, I'm also compensated independently by the coffee shop for sourcing and qualifying a roaster against their brief. This is a separate mandate on their side rather than part of [the roaster's] fee."

**Separate Stripe products, one per service:**

- **Café:** "Sourcing and qualification of a local coffee roasting partner against [Coco]'s stated requirements."
- **Roaster:** "Qualified wholesale account origination."

**His rule if either side objects:** "I'd rather lose the intro than not get paid by both sides in these cases."

**Price:** the café said "up to $3,000," above his $2,497 list. He charged $3,000.

## 5. After payment

**Thesis brief** (sent with the intro), in his description:

- **Match thesis:** "[Company] is seeking a local roasting partner capable of building a differentiated house coffee program, beginning with a custom espresso and preserving a path to private label under the [Coco] name."
- **Requirements and confirmed fits:** each side's own words.
- **A mapped first conversation:** espresso profile → flavor direction → sample iterations → wholesale economics → private label path → launch support.

**Continuation offer to the roaster (exact):**
> "If you want to keep this going after the first introduction, I can roll the $3,000 you already paid into a 60 to 90 day engagement and keep sourcing until we reach the agreed three to six opportunities. I'll send the continuation structure separately."

His reasoning: "The first payment becomes the beginning, not a sunk one-off."

## 6. Decision analysis

| Moment | What he did | Why (his words / **inference**) | Not done |
|---|---|---|---|
| The social-proof line | Deleted it | "You do not need social proof … you're giving them exactly what they want" | Adding testimonials |
| The café offered up to $3K | Charged $3K, not his $2,497 list | **Inference:** the buyer named her own budget, so taking it is fair and frictionless | Negotiating down |
| Both sides pre-sold | Invoiced both, with a transparency line in each | "Be bold." Each side gets a distinct service | Hiding the double payment |
| Moving channels | New threads from his own inbox | "I don't want the invoice payment and the actual introduction sitting inside some random inbox that was built to contact hundreds of people" | Closing from the cold inbox |
| After sending | No follow-up for 24 hours | "We place it and let go … I'm not going to follow up" | Chasing |
| After payment | Thesis brief, then a continuation offer | Reduces buyer's remorse; turns a one-off into a retainer | Disappearing after the intro |

## 8. Rules from this case

**Transferable:**

- Permanent-demand markets need no trigger; every business in them is a live buyer.
- When both sides confirm, invoice both, each for a named, different service, with a transparency line.
- If a buyer offers more than your price, accept it.
- Offer a continuation right after the first delivery, crediting the first payment.
- A one-page thesis brief with a mapped first conversation makes the intro feel professional and reduces remorse.

**Specific to this market:** sampling (it makes the market location-independent), private label.

**Do not copy:**

- "I work with wholesale coffee roasters actively taking on new accounts" and "I have cafés … actively looking" were sent at level 2. They're true in category, not as relationships. Use them only once someone on that side has engaged.
- "Opportunity to send in the next 24 hours, just to add some urgency" (a subject line he used) is manufactured urgency. Avoid it.
