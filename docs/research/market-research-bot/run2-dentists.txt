Dental Practice Market Research

Target audience: Independent dental practice owners, general dentists, and multi-location dental groups in the United States.

The biggest opportunity when marketing to dentists is understanding one important distinction:

Most dentists don't simply want more patients. They want more of the right patients, for the right procedures, without increasing their stress, overhead, or working hours.

A dentist with a full schedule of low-reimbursement procedures can be less profitable than one with fewer appointments and a healthier mix of high-value treatments.

Below is a detailed breakdown of their everyday problems, how they currently attract patients, what frustrates them, and what they secretly want from a marketing company.

These are research-based working hypotheses and realistic examples of dentist language, not verified interview quotes. Individual practices will vary.

1. Problems dentists face
Problem 1: Empty hygienist chairs

"I'm paying my hygienist whether the chair is full or empty. Every cancellation costs me money, and my front desk is scrambling to fill the gaps."

What this looks like:

A hygienist has two openings tomorrow, but nobody is scheduled.

Patients cancel their six-month cleanings at the last minute.

The practice has hundreds of overdue recall patients who haven't been contacted.

The front desk is too busy answering phones to chase unscheduled patients.

What they really want: Two or three overdue patients booked into otherwise empty hygiene slots this week.

Problem 2: Lots of production, not enough owner profit

"We're doing over a million dollars a year, but after payroll, supplies, rent, equipment payments, and insurance adjustments, I'm wondering where all the money went."

What this looks like:

Payroll increases faster than collections.

Insurance reimbursements don't keep up with costs.

Lab fees eat into the profit from crowns and other restorative work.

The owner works clinically four or five days a week but takes home less than expected.

More patients mean more work, but not necessarily more profit.

What they really want: More profitable production per clinical day, not just higher gross revenue.

Problem 3: Not enough implant cases

"I invested in implant training and equipment, but I'm still doing fillings and single crowns all day. Where are the patients who actually need implants?"

What this looks like:

The dentist wants to perform more implant consultations.

The practice gets inquiries from people who can't afford treatment.

Patients ask about implants but disappear when they hear the estimated cost.

High-value consultations are inconsistent.

What they really want: A steady flow of clinically appropriate, financially prepared implant consultations.

Problem 4: Patients who only want the cheapest option

"People call asking how much an implant or crown costs. We explain everything, and then they say they'll call back. We never hear from them again."

Typical tire kickers:

People calling five offices for the lowest implant price.

Patients asking for free consultations without intending to schedule.

People seeking treatment that is entirely covered by insurance.

Cosmetic dentistry inquiries from patients who haven't considered financing.

Prospects who book consultations but never show up.

What they really want: Patients who understand the treatment, appreciate the dentist's expertise, and are prepared to discuss realistic payment options.

Problem 5: Empty spots in the doctor's schedule

"I've got a two-hour opening Thursday afternoon. My assistant is here, the room is ready, and there's no patient."

What this looks like:

A large restorative case cancels.

Treatment plans are accepted but never scheduled.

The office has outstanding treatment opportunities sitting in its practice management software.

The dentist spends valuable chair time on low-production procedures.

What they really want: More accepted treatment plans converted into actual appointments.

Problem 6: Seasonal slowdowns

"January is slow because people are recovering from holiday spending. Then summer comes, families travel, and my schedule gets unpredictable again."

Potential seasonal pressures include:

January insurance deductible resets.

Summer vacations and school breaks.

Holiday cancellations.

Year-end rushes to use insurance benefits.

Local economic fluctuations affecting elective treatments.

What they really want: A predictable pipeline of patients even when routine demand softens.

Problem 7: Patients aren't accepting treatment plans

"We diagnose $80,000 worth of treatment, but only a fraction of those patients actually schedule."

Common reasons include affordability, anxiety, unclear communication, insufficient follow-up, and competing priorities.

What they really want: More patients who understand their recommended care and confidently schedule it.

Problem 8: Too many unscheduled patients sitting in the database

"We have thousands of patient records, but nobody has time to call everyone who is overdue or never finished their treatment."

This is particularly frustrating because the practice has already spent money attracting these patients.

What they really want: Reactivate existing patients without adding another full-time employee.

Problem 9: Expensive equipment isn't being used enough

"I bought a scanner, invested in advanced training, and upgraded the office. Now I need enough patients for those services to justify the investment."

Examples include CBCT scanners, intraoral scanners, CAD/CAM systems, and implant equipment.

What they really want: More demand for procedures that use their existing clinical capabilities.

Problem 10: Dependence on insurance plans

"I'm working harder than ever, but insurance companies keep squeezing my reimbursements."

They may want to reduce dependence on low-reimbursement plans, improve their fee-for-service mix, or attract patients willing to pay for elective services.

What they really want: Better control over their revenue and patient mix.
