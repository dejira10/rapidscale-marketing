# Saad — Calls, Closing, Intros and After

## 1. Routing: is there a real match?

- **Map every reply** by: who replied, what they need, who to connect them to,
  and who pays [S01].
- **Count only matches where both sides said they need each other** in their
  replies. "Could work" doesn't count. "Just because we want to be honest."
  [S02]
- **Use a routing matrix** [S13]:
  - list demand replies (need) against supply replies (offer);
  - match by fit, e.g. a no-MOQ manufacturer for tiny brands;
  - note your alternatives in case one side stalls;
  - estimate the potential, e.g. 4 × $1,997 + 5 × $3,589–6,564.
- **"They literally described each other"** means a qualified match [S08].
- **Verify the supplier before you charge**, using:
  - their website FAQ, roles staffed and project pages;
  - public records of their wholesale operation;
  - a past project's address;
  - their DOT registration;
  - the stated requirements (e.g. 7,000+ ft runway experience).

  [S05, S06, S07, S11]
- **Match geographically**: delivery area, pickup area, same city [S03, S11,
  S15].
- **Reality corrects filters.** "Skip hosts who already have a cleaner" was
  wrong, because a cleaner is throughput, not a subscription [S16].

## 2. Calls: a tool, not a law

- A call is a tool for reducing uncertainty. "A call is just a tool, not a law
  of nature." [S11] The uncertainty is:
  - who are you;
  - can you deliver;
  - what am I paying for;
  - what happens after I pay;
  - what if it doesn't work?

  If you answer those in writing, the call has nothing left to do [S05, S11].
- **"Let the deal tell you whether you need to hop on a call."** If there's
  friction or an objection, take the call. Calls build trust [S06].
- **First calls are short:** 10–20 minutes [S03, S40, S50].

### Supply call (you're interviewing them) [S18, S33]

1. **Open with quiet authority.**
   - "I've been around a small number of {demand} who are actively {need}.
     Before I route anyone your way, I want to understand how you currently
     work with new clients."
   - Name their client's real problem, then ask: "What does your capacity look
     like right now, and what type of clients actually fits how you work?"
     [S18]
2. **Spend about 80% gathering intel**: the client types they want, capacity,
   best engagements, price range, and industries they avoid [S18].
3. **Spend the last 20% on the transition**, if there's a fit. Use the retainer
   pitch in `offers-and-pricing.md` §6.
4. **Open honestly** [S33]: "I'm not a remediation operator myself. I don't run
   cleanups, but I do sit close to a specific category of demand: companies
   moving through EPA enforcement. Is your team typically looking to get in
   front of more enforcement-driven situations?"
5. **Listen for their real economics and re-aim the lane.** The remediation
   firm's real value was recurring waste accounts, and enforcement was only the
   door-opener. So he repositioned: "The better lane is enforcement-driven
   accounts with immediate pressure *and* enough operational activity to turn
   into an MSA or recurring waste stream." It was paid the same day [S33].

### Demand call (role-play example) [S23]

[S23] is a role-play with a community member, not a real client.

- **Opener structure:**
  1. appreciation, calm rather than approval-seeking;
  2. why I'm here;
  3. a market observation;
  4. pull back with a question.

  > "Quick context on why I reached out: I've been around a small number of
  > {suppliers} that work with {demand type}. Before I introduce them, I wanted
  > to understand how you're currently thinking about {topic}, because usually
  > the challenge isn't finding {suppliers}; there are plenty. The harder part
  > is finding one who actually thinks the right way, communicates clearly and
  > deserves trust. So where are you right now: already working with someone,
  > or open to seeing what else is out there?"

- **Position as a filter.** "I'm not here to show you names; you can find those
  yourself. I'm here to filter the difference between those who look credible
  and those who fit how you think."
- **"What am I paying for?"** "You're paying me to get the right firms with the
  capacity for your situation in front of you fast, without wasting time sorting
  through polished wrong fits."
- **Reference requests:** decline to name clients ("same discretion I'd apply to
  yours"). Offer a written summary of the process, the delivery standard, the
  timeline and what counts as a successful introduction.
- **"Which firms are you with?"** Give an honest mix: "I'm not pretending every
  advisor is a close friend. The value is knowing who fits."
- **Don't adopt** the pressure and status moves in [S23]: refusing a proposal,
  "doubling down" when the prospect says you didn't ask about their company. See
  `voice-and-exclusions.md`.

### Who are you? (identity answers) [S39]

Use only what's true:

- **"The researcher":** "I track companies in {industry} that are actively
  {signal} and connect them with the best providers. I noticed you posted
  {signal}…"
- **"The hub":** "Companies call me when their current vendors can't keep up;
  providers call me when they need more clients." Only if it's true.
- Then: "Before I waste your time, let me ask you a few questions."

## 3. The close email (no call needed)

**Move to your own Gmail in a new, clean thread** [S05, S06, S08]: "{first},
taking this over to my proper Gmail so I can send you the opportunity properly."

**Structure** (combined from S05, S06, S07, S09, S11, S12, S16):

1. **Who you are, in one line.** "I run {company}. In very simple terms, I find
   businesses that need {X} and introduce them directly to companies that can
   supply what they're already buying."
2. **The opportunity, described without naming the company**, in their own
   words:
   - "I have a {type} in {place} that {stated need, quoted}."
   - "{Contact role} told me {requirement}."
3. **Why it fits them**, with evidence you verified:
   - "From what I found on {company}, you've already done {relevant work}, so
     you looked like an obvious company to put in front of them." [S06]
   - "Your {case study} work is why I came to you first." [S17]
4. **ROI framing when the price could feel arbitrary.** "This isn't a one-off
   clean. It's her ongoing turnover work, week after week." [S16]
5. **The fee, stated plainly, with the invoice**:
   - "My fee for the qualified introduction is ${X}. Invoice is attached."
   - Or "The way I'm structuring this is ${X} for the introduction."
6. **Exactly what they get** [S05, S07, S11, S12]:

   > "I'll introduce you both directly by email with the full context of
   > {their need}. You'll have their name, company and direct contact details
   > and can discuss {pricing, volumes, schedule} directly. That's my entire
   > delivery. No meetings or calls needed with me."

   Make the deliverable concrete enough to picture.
7. **Replacement clause.** "If {they} materially don't match what I described,
   I'll replace the introduction."
8. **Next step.** "Once payment is taken care of, I'll make the introduction
   immediately" or "within the next 24 hours."

Optional lines:

- **A true answer** to "what's in it for you?" [S07].
- **Scarcity, only if true.** "I do have a few other production teams I can
  route this to, so if you don't want it that's fine." [S17]
- **The transparency line** when both sides pay [S08].
- **Answer the skeptic's question first.** "Quick clarification: I'm not
  representing a sub and I'm not selling you data. I find {buyers} with
  upcoming work, qualify what they're looking for, and charge the {seller} for
  the direct introduction." [S06]

**Don't overclaim.** He admitted that two of his close lines were stretches:

- "I spend a lot of time around K-12 technology," after one hour of research
  [S09];
- "basically what I do is…" when he "started doing this today" [S12].

Describe your experience truthfully.

### Keep the other side warm while you close [S05, S06, S07, S09, S11, S14]

- "Thank you for clarifying the requirements. I have one {type} that looks like
  it could fit what you described. I'm checking them now against {requirements}.
  If everything lines up, I'll introduce you directly today."
- "There's no cost on your side. My arrangement is with the {other side}."
- **Repeat-source line** [S09]:

  > "If this is useful, feel free to send me future {category} requests where
  > you'd like another qualified company looking at it. I can screen them on my
  > side before they ever reach you."

  This turns the buyer into a recurring source of deals.
- **Standing contact on a scarce supplier** [S15]:

  > "For future {buyers} I send you, give me one person on your team I can
  > message directly to tell me two things quickly: can you {serve this area},
  > and roughly how fast are {results} turning right now?"

- **Reframe questions you can't answer yet** [S07]: "You mentioned you wanted to
  know their minimum. This is actually why I think you could be a good fit."

### Payment

- **Stripe or any processor** (PayPal, Payoneer, Wise). Make a separate product
  for each side [S08, S17].
- **Product names are high-status**, with a description that lists the
  deliverable, the timing and the replacement clause [S14, S16]. See
  `offers-and-pricing.md` §7.
- **Example description** [S14]: "6–9 qualified opportunities matched to
  product, geography, license and compliance; the first released on payment and
  fit confirmation, the rest over 60–90 days; replacement clause."

## 4. The qualified thesis brief

Send it with the intro [S08], or with the invoice before revealing the intro
[S50].

**One page** [S08]:

- **Match thesis:** what the buyer seeks.
- **The buyer's stated requirements**, in their words.
- **The supplier's confirmed fit**, in their words.
- **A mapped first-conversation agenda**, e.g. espresso profile → flavor
  direction → sample iterations → wholesale economics → private label path →
  launch support.
- **Footer:** "Qualified introduction by {company}, {month year}."
- Write it in institutional language. It reduces buyer's remorse and becomes a
  proof document.

**Penalty-lane version** [S50]:

- signal date;
- urgency (x/5);
- "lane fit" grade;
- a reframe of the stakes, e.g. "the penalty is the small number; the exposure
  is the hazard staying half fixed";
- why this supplier fits: third-party credibility, specialization, industry
  depth.

His prompt: "Take a match between a demand-side company and a supply-side
provider, then write a sharp operator-grade thesis explaining why the match
matters."

**He wrote these by hand at first because the AI "was inventing stuff."** Check
every fact in an AI-drafted thesis against the sources [S50].

**Attachments that help** [S09, S14]:

- RFP documents;
- the supplier's product and pricing file, turned into a clean Google Sheet;
- verified documentation checked against the buyer's requirements.

## 5. The introduction email

**Workhorse template** (use it about 80% of the time) [S40]:

> Subject: Intro — quick context
>
> Hi {A}, {B} — looping you both in based on timing.
>
> {Company A} recently {signal: raised / hired / launched / expanded}, which
> typically triggers {relevant activity}. At the same time, {Company B} works
> closely with teams at this exact stage, specifically around {capability}.
>
> Figured it made sense to connect you two and let you decide if there's a
> conversation worth having. I'll leave it to you both from here.

Why it works: why now, why these two, no pressure.

**Full-context version** (after a paid match) [S05, S07, S08, S11]:

- **Subject:** "{A} / {B} — {context}", or "Intro — direct introduction from
  {you}."
- **Body:**
  - who each is;
  - what each told you, including their numbers (volumes, dogs a week, kWh,
    roles short);
  - why it's an obvious match;
  - "You've both already confirmed the core commercial fit with me, so there
    shouldn't be much need to requalify from zero" [S08];
  - "I'll let you take it from here on {scope, pricing, schedule, paperwork}."
- **Carry full context** so neither side restarts the conversation.
- **Include each side's key questions**, such as MSA status or rates [S12].
- **Don't mention the fee** in the intro [S12].
- **After payment:** "Got it, payment came through. I'm introducing you to {them}
  right now." [S17]

## 6. After the intro (the middle path)

You're neither disappearing nor becoming their project manager [S17].

1. **Set a ~24-hour reminder.** If you've heard nothing, send one human message
   at about 7am their time: "Hey {first}, hope you're well. How did the
   conversation with {B} go?"
2. **Classify the outcome:**
   - Did they speak?
   - Did they like each other?
   - Are they discussing work?

   If yes, leave them alone, and check back a few days later.
3. **Don't over-deliver.** "The value is often in knowing what not to add."
   [S15] Don't negotiate rates or manage their schedule [S16].
4. **Help if needed, and replace if it's not a fit.** He claims a ~2%
   replacement rate [S08].
5. **Track lightly** [S17]: who was introduced, when, whether they spoke and
   liked each other, whether it became a project, its approximate value, and
   whether it's usable as a case study. He uses Linear. Don't build a CRM before
   your first 3–5 customers.
6. **Repeat sale:** "Hey, got another one that looks like your kind of thing.
   Want me to send it over?" [S17]
7. **Free-side case-study ask** (put it in the warm email, and send it again
   after the project closes) [S10, S17]:

   > "There's no fee or obligation on your side. The only favor I ask: if the
   > introduction works well for you, would you be able to send me a quick
   > 30-second phone video afterwards saying what you were looking for and how
   > the introduction went? Nothing polished at all."

8. **Continuation or retainer offer** to the paying side. See
   `offers-and-pricing.md` §6.

## 7. Personal brand as the moat [S02]

"Selling access is a business; being known as the person who does it is the
moat." Three habits:

1. Publish what you learn twice a week for a year on LinkedIn or YouTube
   ("here's what I saw this week").
2. Attend the industry's physical events where both sides meet, once a year.
3. Keep a private directory of patterns, and publish a free quarterly "state of
   the industry" on your site.

The outcome: referrals from both sides. "You become infrastructure."
