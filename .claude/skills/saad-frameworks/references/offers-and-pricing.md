# Saad — Offers, Pricing and Positioning

## 1. What you're selling

- **Access, timing and priority, not introductions.** "The introduction is just
  how it gets delivered." If you think you sell intros, you'll feel guilty and
  undercharge [S40].
  - Restaurants sell experience, not food.
  - Netflix sells Friday nights, not videos.
  - Uber sells not waiting in the rain, not rides.
- **Not AI, automation, booked meetings, appointment setting or lead lists.**
  Both sides have heard those pitches and say no [S01]. Why he stopped selling
  meetings [S03]:
  - you promise that another human will show up, which you don't control;
  - you end up chasing no-shows and arguing about "qualified";
  - it puts you in the lead-gen box next to 50 others.
- **"You get paid for removing stupidity from both ends."** The buyer can
  Google 40 labs but can't tell who's competent [S01].
- **Distinguish yourself from lead gen.** "These are not random lists or public
  opportunities. The value is routing live situations." [S33] "Are you just
  selling a list? No, you're getting actual qualified conversations." [S02,
  S13]
- **One-line positioning** (child-simple):
  - "I find businesses that need customers and put them in front of businesses
    that want to sell to them." [S10, S11]
  - "I track new [enforcement events] and connect [companies] in active
    [remediation] windows with specialists who can stabilize the site fast."
    [S41]
  - Website: "research and introductions," "quiet authority," published theses
    and current signals ("email me if any of these are yours"), and "currently
    accepting two new counterparties this quarter" [S50].

## 2. The two-sided offer templates

**Supply side (they pay for access to opportunities):**

> "For the next 60 to 90 days, I'll bring you three to six [demand description,
> e.g. certified food makers] that we've contacted and confirmed are open to
> [discussing what you sell]. You pay from $X to $Y for access to these
> opportunities. If I bring you somebody that clearly does not fit, I replace
> them." [S01, S02, S03, S04, S13]

**Demand side (they pay for sourcing):**

> "I'll find you two to three [suppliers] that actually fit what you need,
> speak with them, qualify them and introduce you to the best ones. You pay me
> $X for the sourcing." [S01, S02, S04, S13]

Variants:

- **Samples first:** "…and you'll have samples sent directly to you so you can
  taste the coffee before choosing anybody." [S08]
- **The pharmacy version:** "I'll find you two to three licensed backup
  wholesalers that can supply medicine your normal wholesaler either doesn't
  have, keeps putting on backorder, or is charging too much for." [S14]

Answer these in the offer before they're asked [S02, S13]:

- "Why are they early?" → "They filed recently."
- "How do you know they need X?" → "We spoke to them and qualified that before
  introducing them."
- "How do you know they want America?" → "They joined export programs, came to
  the US, and named the US as a market they want."

Notice the offer doesn't say "we'll generate your leads." [S04]

## 3. The guarantee: replacement only

- "If I bring you somebody that clearly does not fit, I replace them." Or: "If
  the [opportunity] materially differs from what I described, I'll replace the
  introduction at no additional cost." [S01, S05, S06, S11]
- **"You're not guaranteeing the weather, you're selling umbrellas."** A broken
  umbrella gets replaced [S01].
- **"Your guarantee should stay exactly where your work ends."** [S13]
  - Never guarantee meetings, show-ups, chemistry or a closed deal.
  - Watermelon example: bring a banana buyer and you owe another. Bring a real
    watermelon buyer and the shopkeeper insults him? You don't owe another.
- **No refunds.** "I've never given refunds; I only replace introductions."
  [S10]
- **Paid before delivery, non-negotiable.** The replacement promise makes
  paying first comfortable [S04].
- **Separate what you control from what you don't** [S23].
  - You control: filtering, qualification, the capacity of who gets in the
    room.
  - You don't control: chemistry, markets, the relationship.
  - "Delivery standard": "If they clearly lack the capacity, that's my failure
    and I'll address it."
- **Contract language** [S40]: "[Company] agrees to make commercially reasonable
  efforts to facilitate introductions. [Company] does not guarantee acceptance,
  engagement, or transaction completion."
- **Bundle a window, not one intro.** E.g. "we'll activate this for 30 days," or
  30–90 days. One no or one ignore doesn't break delivery [S40].
- **Earlier agency-era version** of risk reversal (no refund talk): "This is
  built to work, and I stay involved until it does." [S35] This doesn't fit the
  connector doctrine. Prefer replacement.

## 4. Pricing from first principles

**Four questions** [S15]:

1. How bad is the problem?
2. How badly do they need it gone?
3. How hard is it for them to get what I'm bringing?
4. What is being blocked (revenue, harvest, opening, contract)?

**Price on the value, not the work.** "Price on the money trapped behind the
problem." "Once you think like this you'll 10x your prices." [S15, S16]

**Anchor on their economics** and show the math:

- Airport paving: ~$1.2M contract at 11–16% margin ≈ $130–195k per job, so
  $8,600 [S06].
- E-Rate: ~$61k a year × 86% renewal × ~7 years ≈ $433k lifetime value, so
  $10k "looks cheap" [S09].
- Nursing home: ≥$95k a year to an agency, so $8k [S11].
- Oil: $5–50M contracts, so $6,500 [S12].
- Wholesaler: compare to $6,500 trade-show booths and $14,300 private meeting
  rooms [S14].
- Recurring cleaning account: worth a multiple of one job [S16].
- "Your fee is the smallest number in the room." [S38]

**Other pricing rules:**

- **Non-round numbers look calculated** ($2,589, $3,589, $1,997). They pre-empt
  "where did that number come from?" [S01, S46]
- **Price for the first deal.** Make it high enough to be good money, low
  enough to need no proof. "I want the opportunity itself to do the selling, not
  my reputation." Raise it with proof [S05].
- **No formula.** "There's no formula; pretending otherwise is one of the most
  common lies in this space." He picked $3,000 [S16] and $4,000 [S10] by
  judgment.
- **Bigger buyers pay more**, roughly 10× for a big brand [S04]. In biotech, a
  price under ~$4–8k looks illegitimate [S22, S45].
- **If a buyer offers more than your price, take it.** Don't negotiate down
  [S08].
- **Shorter timelines cost more.** "Same work, shorter clock means sharper
  focus, not cheaper work." $15k for 60–90 days became $20k for priority 30
  days [S23].
- **Splits are fine; lowering the price isn't.** He accepted $10k upfront plus
  $10k on the first qualified conversation [S23].
- **He sometimes underprices.** A buyer's account was later estimated at ~$188k
  a month on a $12k engagement [S14]. Post-mortem every deal [S05].

## 5. Charging both sides

- **Each side pays for a different service** [S08, S37]:
  - Supply buys priority access, first shot, pre-filtered opportunities, and
    savings on SDRs and ads.
  - Demand buys faster sourcing of the right supplier plus a warm intro that
    gets answered. Sometimes they also pay a % of the closed deal ("bounty
    hunter").
- **No conflict between the two** [S40]:
  - Supply didn't buy a specific company or exclusivity.
  - Demand didn't buy a promise that suppliers won't also pay.
  - "Side A pays to know who needs help first; side B pays to skip the search
    and get to the right ones fast. Never sell exclusivity; never promise the
    same result."
- **Be transparent.** Include this line in each close email [S08]:

  > "For transparency, I'm also compensated independently by [the other side]
  > for [the service you provide them]. This is a separate mandate on their
  > side rather than part of your fee."

  - Name the services separately, e.g. "sourcing and qualification of wholesale
    coffee roaster" vs. "qualified wholesale account origination."
  - Never describe it as "charging both sides for the intro."
  - If one side refuses because the other pays: "I'd rather lose the intro than
    not get paid by both sides in these cases." [S08]
  - **Don't follow [S39]**'s "they don't need to know."
- **One side free** is common. Tell the free side: "There's no fee or obligation
  on your side; my arrangement is with [the other company]." [S07, S09, S11,
  S12]
- **After a supplier buys a 3–6 opportunity batch**, extra matches bill only the
  demand side [S01].
- **Optional upside** after the first order closes: ask the free side for a
  small optional % of ongoing orders, e.g. 2% [S07]. Ask only after they close.

## 6. Retainers, windows and continuation

- **Continuation** [S08]: "If you want to keep this going after the first
  introduction, I can roll the $3,000 you already paid into a 60 to 90 day
  engagement and keep sourcing until we reach the agreed three to six
  opportunities."
- **Supply-side priority routing retainers**: ~$5–9k a month, first intros in
  60–90 days [S18], or $5–8k a month [S50].
  - He mentions a 6-month minimum for his retainer product [S50].
  - He claims clients stay ~1.5 years [S18].
- **Retainer pitch at the end of a supply call** [S18]:

  > "Based on what you just told me, you're exactly the type of operator I'd
  > route demand to. Here's how the model works: I charge a monthly retainer to
  > keep you in priority routing. When something fits, I send it your way
  > before anything else. I'm actively building the demand pipeline right now.
  > First introductions typically come within 60 to 90 days. If that timeline
  > works for you, we can start now."

- **Sell the system, not the intro** [S39]. "One intro is fine, but your goals
  require a system that creates opportunities every week." That moves you from
  one-off intros to retainers. Pitch each side its own version: factories buy
  speed and reliability; staffing firms buy client acquisition.
- **Hybrid structures** [S39]:
  - hands-off: setup fee plus a small rev share;
  - hands-on: retainer plus a % of closed deals.
- **Recruitment flywheel** [S40]: access fees of $3–6k on each side, then a
  10–15% rev share on placements.
- **No rev-share-only or pay-per-close for small suppliers.** You do all the
  work and may never get paid [S18]. Instead:
  - go upmarket to firms that already paid for marketing that didn't work;
  - or offer a "90-day trial at a reduced rate" with a step-up. Never call it a
    discount.
- **Two products** [S50]: a 6-month retainer, and high-ticket one-off intros.

## 7. Language discipline

Payment doesn't equal representation. "Payment equals access to your
infrastructure." [S40]

- **Never say** "my clients," "we represent them," "they're paying us," or "I'm
  working on their behalf."
- **Say** "a company we're activating," "a team entering growth mode," "a
  company that just hit a scaling signal," "a role that just opened up," or "a
  situation that just triggered."
- **To demand:** "We maintain a vetted supply pool that opts into growth-stage
  demand. When companies hit this stage, we coordinate access to operators who
  specialize in it."
- **To the free side:** "I do this independently. I don't represent them." [S07,
  S11, S12]
- **Avoid naming yourself** "marketing services," "lead generation" or
  "appointment setting." Use high-status names [S14]:
  - "Texas Pharmacy Account Origination: 60–90 Day Engagement";
  - "Qualified Illinois airport introduction";
  - "Qualified Airbnb Cleaning Customer Introduction."
- **Use "institutional language"** (a private-banking tone) at the commercial
  stage, and casual language in cold outreach [S08, S09].

## 8. Inventory decisions

**Three-bucket filter** for "we want X clients" [S18]:

1. **Inside.** It's already in your demand inventory, maybe under different
   words. Sign them.
2. **Adjacent.** It's a sub-segment of the same lane. Sign them, and be honest:
   "I can tune my outreach to capture more of X; give me one to three weeks."
3. **Outside.** It's a different lane. Decline:

   > "I think what you need is real and I think there's someone who can help
   > with that, but that's not what my system is built to deliver. I'd rather
   > be honest with you now than take your money for three months."

   - Route them to a peer.
   - "I route from flow. I don't build custom pipes for one person." The urge to
     "spin up a campaign" for them is the old freelancer identity.

**Asymmetric stocking** (one side replies, the other is silent) is information,
not a problem. There are three possible causes [S18]:

- the quiet side's outbound is wrong, which you can diagnose in an hour from
  reply rates;
- that side is structurally slower (board approvals), so give it more time and
  volume;
- the niche is supply-heavy, so demand has pricing power. Don't overstock
  supply.

**When supply is ahead of demand** [S18]:

- close supply with a transparent longer lead time ("first introduction 60–90
  days, not 30");
- close them at a reduced retainer that steps up after the first success;
- or keep them warm for 4–6 weeks while you crack demand, then close at full
  price.

**Every conversation on one side is prospecting for the other side.** You never
start from zero [S02].

**The flywheel:** every new supplier makes demand more valuable, and vice versa
[S40].
