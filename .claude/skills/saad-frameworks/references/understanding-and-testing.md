# Saad — How He Understands a Market, and How He Tests It

`markets.md` covers *which* markets he picks. This file covers what he does
next:

- how he learns a market he knows nothing about (Part A);
- what he watches for when he tests it with real emails (Part B);
- how to tell whether to keep going, re-aim or drop it (Part C).

His stance is "dumb maxing": move fast and learn by touching reality. "More
knowledge won't help you." [S09] "Only look for knowledge when reality asks you
for it." [S12]

---

## Part A — How he understands a market

### A1. Follow the money: who already pays whom, and how much

Before anything else, map the money that's already moving.

- **Who's forced to pay?** Laws, inspections, certification, batch testing,
  public penalties [S01, S15, S33].
- **What does the supply side already spend to get customers?** [S03, S14, S16]
  - Trade-show booths: $2,750–5,000 for space at daycare conferences [S03];
    $6,500 booths and $14,300 private meeting rooms for wholesalers [S14].
  - Billboards, Google Ads, Meta ads [S03, S16].
  - Brokers already used by oilfield crews and nursing agencies [S11, S12].
- **What is one customer worth to them?** Contract size × margin, renewal rate
  × years [S06, S09, S11]. For example:
  - a $1.2M paving contract at 11–16% margin ≈ $130–195k per job [S06];
  - E-Rate at $61k a year × 86% renewal × ~7 years ≈ $433k [S09].
- **What is blocked until the problem is solved?** A harvest that can't be
  sold, a launch that's delayed, beds left empty, a facility shut down [S11,
  S15, S50].
- **"Money backwards law":** prospects only care if it makes them money, or
  saves time that turns into money [S07].

### A2. Ask "what had to happen before this?"

Keep descending from the visible event to the hidden one that moves the money
[S13]. TikTok product → manufacturer → founder choosing a manufacturer →
trademark filed. "A beauty brand is born on paper before it's born in a
bottle." The earliest public trace is where you want to stand.

### A3. Translate the signal into the pain it creates

A signal is "something that just changed within a company." Ask what problem
that change creates, who pays to solve it, and who can solve it [S49]. Use the
signal-to-pain table in `markets.md` §2. Stack two pains on one company when
you can, e.g. fresh funding plus a new marketing leader [S58].

### A4. Read the public data like a story

The data tells you who, when and how urgent:

- a license date means the business just opened [S03];
- a lost bid means capacity and the need for the next job [S06];
- 37 open job posts means "literally in pain" [S49];
- a warning letter with no response filed means the clock is running [S41];
- contract-staff hours on a nursing home's payroll report mean it already pays
  agencies [S11];
- Gmail or Yahoo contacts mean nobody pitches them [S03, S10].

"The money is in the looking. The barrier was never really access. The barrier
is doing the boring work." [S06]

### A5. Learn the pain in their own words

- **Read where owners complain.** For pharmacies, he read Reddit threads from
  independent pharmacy owners about their primary wholesaler, and copied their
  wording into the offer [S14].
- **Read the supplier's own site**: FAQ, roles staffed, case studies, project
  pages. That tells you what they actually sell and to whom [S05, S11, S17].
- **Read the documents**: RFPs, warning letters, permits [S09, S41].
- **Look it up as it comes up.** Google every industry term you don't know when
  it appears in a reply [S05, S12].

### A6. Let the replies teach you the market

Replies are research, not just leads.

- **What prospects ask shows what matters:**
  - "Who are the buyers?"
  - "What are they buying?"
  - "Show me the sellers."
  - "I can buy, but there's a rule around who I'm allowed to buy from."

  Nobody raised an impossible objection [S04].
- **What they offer shows what they fear.** A buyer offering cash-in-advance
  fears running out more than price [S07].
- **What they already tried shows how bad it is.** One nursing home had raised
  rates, offered referral bonuses and tried same-day pay [S11].
- **A supplier's real economics can differ from your theory.** The remediation
  firm wanted recurring waste accounts, and enforcement was only the way in.
  So he re-aimed the lane [S33].
- **Reality corrects your filters.** A host who already had a cleaner still
  needed more capacity [S16].

### A7. Use other sources of insight

- **Your own life.** Needing dog boarding [S10], a pharmacy out of melatonin
  [S14], owning a café [S08].
- **A daily reading habit.** He reads newsletters and annual reports every
  morning and looks for "two markets that needed each other." [S41]
- **The rule underneath it all.** "Do people actually need what I sell?" [S03]
  Start from what both sides want. Don't start from what you can sell [S02].

## Part B — What he looks for when testing a new market

### B1. How he runs the test

- **Email both sides at the same time.** That puts "forces on both ends of the
  same transaction" [S16]. Every conversation on one side becomes prospecting
  for the other (the "Lollapalooza effect," where you're never starting from
  zero) [S02].
- **Keep it small.**
  - About 100 per side [S05, S07].
  - About 50–65 high-intent records can be enough to close 1–3 [S39, S41].
  - "Volume is not king." [S38]
  - 100 × 100 is 10,000 possible pairs, and you need one [S10].
- **Use plain copy**, so the market's response is about the opportunity, not
  your cleverness [S04, S05].
- **Test small first, then scale.** The "Darwinian mental model": test, observe,
  fine-tune [S36].
- **Count only two numbers:** reply rates and actual money made [S60].
- **Then step away.** "Place it and then let go." Don't refresh the inbox [S02].

### B2. Positive signals (the market is "talking back")

| Signal | What it means | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Replies within minutes or a few hours | "The market effectively talking back," a live need nobody else is hitting | [S01, S39, S50] |
| Most positive replies come from the **first** email, not the follow-ups | His hypothesis: a strong market answers step 1, and a medium market answers only to follow-ups | [S17] |
| Prospects start "doing labor": clarifying needs, giving volumes, proposing times, sending invites | "When interest is real, prospects start doing labor" | [S41] |
| Detailed qualifying questions (regions, case types, direct vs. brokered) | He puts it at ~60% likely to close. "They are serious." | [S33] |
| They ask how you get paid, or offer a fee ("Whatever finder's fee or cuts you need on this, just let me know," "we're used to paying this kind of brokerage") | Already used to paying; pre-sold | [S08, S09, S10, S12] |
| "What good timing" | Likely to pay | [S17] |
| They ask for the call themselves | Interest is real | [S33, S50] |
| Both sides describe each other in their replies | A real match: "they literally described each other" | [S08] |
| Skeptical but engaged replies ("What's your actual angle? Just tell me your fee") | A good prospect. Answer plainly | [S06] |
| Testing replies ("Are you selling leads?") | They're checking your positioning, not saying no. Hold frame | [S50] |

### B3. Warning signals

| Signal | What to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Replies are dead on both sides | It's a messaging problem, not a market problem. Fix the copy and targeting. "You can diagnose this pretty much in like an hour." | [S18] |
| One side replies, the other is silent | Find out which it is: wrong outbound, a structurally slower side (more time and volume), or a supply-heavy niche (don't overstock supply) | [S18] |
| Replies come only from follow-ups | Possibly a medium market (his hypothesis) | [S17] |
| More and more qualifying questions without commitment | Ghosting risk. Answer directly and move to the close | [S11] |
| A supplier ghosts after a good call | They don't believe the demand is real. Come back with a real signal, not "checking in" | [S18] |
| The supplier wants rev share only and won't pay upfront | Go upmarket, or offer a 90-day trial at a reduced rate. Don't work for maybe | [S18] |
| The ask is outside your lane | Decline honestly. Don't build custom pipes | [S18] |

### B4. When the test has done its job

- **When both sides confirm, stop researching and push the transaction.** More
  data-pulling kills deals, because the buyer solves it themselves in a week or
  two [S15, S16].
- **If something is publicly verifiable, verify it yourself** rather than
  asking. Asking stalls the deal [S12].
- **Run a post-mortem every time**: why did it work, what did the replies
  teach, was the price right? [S05, S14, S16]

## Part C — Deciding: keep, re-aim or drop

### C1. Do the scale math

Count the actual people who have to say yes. For example, $30k a month =

- 4 supply-side sales at $3,589, plus
- 8 demand-side sales at $1,997,
- which is 12 sales, or about one supplier and two buyers a week.

"That's all what scale is, it's not some mystical hidden secret." [S01] Another
version: $30k = 3 distributors + 3 brands [S02].

### C2. Keep going if

- replies came fast and from the first email [S17, S50];
- at least one side asked about payment, or accepted the price [S08, S10];
- you could make a real match today, where both sides said they need each other
  [S02, S03];
- the routing matrix shows enough potential across both sides [S13].

### C3. Re-aim if

- the suppliers' real economics point to a better lane [S33];
- reply patterns show your filter was wrong [S16];
- one side is structurally slower or supply-heavy (adjust volume and pricing
  power) [S18].

### C4. Drop or park it if

- nobody is in front of you who could pay. "Does this put me in front of
  someone who could pay me? If yes, do it. If not, kill it." [S09]

### C5. Then go deep on one lane

- **One niche is enough** to reach $30–40k a month (his claim) [S38]. "Be a
  goblin": find one simple thing that works and keep hitting the same rock
  [S12].
- **Re-run the data weekly** for new triggers (the "money loop") [S03, S09,
  S14].
- **Build the moat**: publish what you see, attend the industry's events, and
  keep a pattern directory [S02].

## Market-test brief (output for `saad-market-research`)

1. **The lane.** Demand ↔ supply, and the money already moving (A1).
2. **The hidden event** found with "what had to happen before this?" (A2), and
   the signal-to-pain translation (A3).
3. **Trigger data:** source, fields, freshness and volume (A4).
4. **The pain in their words**, with links (A5), and industry vocabulary.
5. **Test design:**
   - the list size for each side;
   - the first email for each side (`outreach.md`);
   - the follow-up steps;
   - the dates.
6. **What to watch:** the B2 and B3 signals, and the decision rules from Part C.
7. **The price hypothesis** from first principles (`offers-and-pricing.md` §4).
   Mark it **suggested**.
8. **After the test:**
   - replies by side and by step;
   - signals seen;
   - matches you could make today;
   - the decision (keep, re-aim or drop) and why.
