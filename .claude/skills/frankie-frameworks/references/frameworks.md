## Core philosophy
- He sells without routine sales calls or client meetings. Short clarification
  calls (about 6 minutes) are rare exceptions, after the prospect has already
  seen the offer and price. [C1 → D2, D3]
- He sells more through text, chat, email and Google Docs than through Loom.
  He says he mostly runs sales conversations by SMS, email and Facebook
  Messenger. [C1 → D11] He confirms the same method works written up in a
  Google Doc instead of a video. [C1 → D12] He taught text-only closing as
  early as 2023. [C1 → D5]
- The real skill is getting the prospect to agree with a plan before asking
  them to buy a service. Loom is just one way to present the plan. [C1 → D11, D12]
- "Most people struggle not because they don't know how to close, but because
  they don't know how to open." [C1 → D11]
- Presentation in three things: demonstrate a result, show a believable plan,
  make an easy, low-risk offer. [C1 → D2]

## Who to target
- Proven buyers: businesses already spending money on relevant services.
  [C1 → D2–D5] Some prospects aren't buyers at all, so target those already
  willing to spend on marketing (Hedy/realtors example). [C1 → D6]
- Prefer owners already in a relevant conversation, such as a trade-community
  post asking whether anyone has used a marketing service. Enter the
  conversation they're already having. [C1 → D11, George coaching]
- Past purchasers can be valuable prospects (Jimmy example). [C1 → D12]

## Market research and problem discovery
- **30,000-foot vs. street-level.** Prospects describe problems vaguely
  ("We need more members"). Dig until you reach the specific, recognizable
  problem ("Our Tuesday class had only two people in it last week…", the yoga
  studio example). This also turns a vague complaint into a solvable problem
  with a clear finish line. [C1 → D13]
- **Hell Island / Sharks / Heaven Island.** Hell Island is the problem they live
  with now, Heaven Island is the outcome they want, and the Sharks are what
  stands in the way (past failures, bad lead quality, distrust of agencies).
  Marketers oversell Heaven and under-research Hell. [C1 → D13]
- **Finger pointer.** Describe the problem as a scene the buyer recognizes:
  the roofer drives through traffic to quote a tire-kicker and loses the job to
  someone cheaper. Name the actual disliked task, not "save time and money"
  (gutter guards: dragging out the ladder). [C1 → D15, D16]
- The useful offer may cut waste rather than add volume. Russ/dentist:
  consults fell from about 75% to 50% after losing a coordinator, so the fix
  was filtering out patients who shouldn't take a consult slot. [C1 → D13, D14]
  Lana: stop adding leads to a process that can't convert the ones it has.
  [C1 → D13]

## Offer and positioning
- **Handraiser first.** Test the offer in 1–3 sentences to see whether
  prospects want it before building the full presentation. [C1 → D2–D5]
- Lead with removing a disliked task, not vaguely "more money" (Shaz).
  [C1 → D13]
- **Small test before the full system**: one test post (Jonathan), a limited
  reactivation campaign (George), conservative measurable tests rather than
  promising dozens of bookings. [C1 → D11, D14]
- **Coffee date → first date → marriage**: small free value → low-risk paid
  test → ongoing service. [C1 → D17]
- Don't invent a fancy name for a standard service. Admit what it is (SEO, PPC)
  and look into why past agencies failed. [C1 → D13, John]
- Be honest when an offer is new or untested, and invite a small experiment.
  [C1 → D11, D7]
- For a first client, value testing and owning a reusable system over getting
  the highest fee. [C1 → D13, Russ]
- Specialize in a niche so language and offers become repeatable (Jonathan).
  [C1 → D11]

## Opening conversations
- The first message is a handraiser, not a pitch. Its only job is to get a
  reply. [C1 → D12]
- **Inbound openers** (credited to the training):
  "What's happening in your world that made you want to reach out?" /
  "What had you reaching out today?" / "Do you mind telling me a little bit
  about your situation and what you're looking to do?" (his favorite) /
  "What are you trying to accomplish?" [C1 → D11]
- **Outbound structure**: a real observation ("Saw you…", "Noticed you…"),
  then a relevant problem or opportunity, then an easy question. No
  manufactured compliments. [C1 → D11, D12]
- **Two opener types**: curiosity/symptom ("Do you ever have…?") when exploring
  whether a problem exists, and idea/permission ("Had an idea for you…") when
  you already see an opportunity. Neither must come first. Don't stitch
  different people's scripts together. [C1 → D12]
- **Tiny reply**: his welcome email asks what kind of work subscribers do, and
  a three-letter reply like "PPC" counts as a successful opening. [C1 → D12]
- **Golf analogy**: use a different club depending on how close they are to
  the hole. Someone ready to buy may just need one answer and a payment link,
  not another presentation. [C1 → D12]
- **Barbecue test**: would you actually say this to someone at a barbecue?
  [C1 → D12]
- Opening high-level: start with a simple idea and add detail only as interest
  grows. [C1 → D11–D13]

## Follow-up questions (discovery)
- Ask one question, listen, and let the answer decide the next one. Don't
  run a fixed survey. [C1 → D11–D13]
- **The parrot**: repeat the key phrase back ("More leads?", "Losing good
  jobs?") so they expand on it. [C1 → D5]
- Question categories C1 found in the training: opening (why now), desired
  outcome, clarification ("More leads? What kind?"), street-level symptoms,
  frequency/scale, impact/cost, previous attempts ("What have you tried so
  far?"), plan agreement ("What do you think of that plan?"). [C1 → D5, D11, D13]
- Always find out what they've tried. A prospect burned by an SEO agency may
  reject the next SEO pitch before hearing what's different. [C1 → D11, D13]
- When "not happy with our agency" comes up, find out exactly what's wrong
  before writing a proposal (Kevin). Discovery can run over several emails.
  [C1 → D5]

## Sales process (text-first)
1. Find out why they're interested. 2. Go deeper into the problem. 3. Find out
what they've tried. 4. Share the plan and get agreement (red light/green light).
5. Introduce working together: implementation, cost, how to start. Some inbound
conversations do this in 3–4 paragraphs. [C1 → D11]

**Red light / green light**: share one piece, then check for understanding or
agreement. Green means continue. Red means resolve the disagreement first,
because a document can't fix a fundamental disagreement about the solution.
Ask permission before sending prerecorded material. Don't answer a small
signal of interest with a huge explanation, whether written by AI or a person.
[C1 → D12]

## Closing in Loom / Google Doc presentation
Original flow: handraiser → brief qualifying chat → 5-minute Loom → questions,
decision, payment → onboarding. [C1 → D2–D5]

Five presentation sections [C1 → D4, D9, D10, D20]:
1. **Dramatic demonstration**: a symptom, outcome, or before-and-after.
2. **Success criteria / ideal client**: 1–3 clear yes/no conditions.
3. **The plan**: 3–5 simple steps with visuals. It must be a *clearly
   different and desirable plan* versus what they've tried; this is where most
   of the selling happens.
4. **Offer (IVQ)**: Investment, Value, Qualifier. Price is always shown with
   the value and who it fits.
5. **Next steps**: exactly how to accept, decline or ask questions.

- **Build in reverse**: next action → price → offer → plan → demonstration,
  then present in forward order. [C1 → D2, D3]
- The Loom presents a plan, not a biography. [C1 → D3–D5]
- Have 5–10 real text conversations before turning it into a reusable
  presentation; customize the first 5–10, then reuse what works. [C1 → D5]
- Lead with results over deliverables; describe exactly what "chasing" looks
  like for the buyer (Jonathan's doc review). [C1 → D11]
- Keep the mechanism simple: it supports the promise rather than burying it.
  [C1 → D11]

## Objection handling
- Repeated questions mean the presentation or offer needs fixing; answer them
  earlier in the doc. [C1 → D6] Sending someone back to the same doc doesn't
  answer their question (Hedy: realtors asking whether investors are in their
  area). [C1 → D6]
- Prevent objections in the offer itself: explain price differences and make
  onboarding simple (Kevin/Lindsay incumbent-agency case). [C1 → D6]
- Treat skepticism as something to look into, not to overpower. [C1 → D13]
- A discount alone doesn't win a provider who is comparing agencies (Elaine).
  [C1 → D6]

## Voice, tone and style
- Conversational, irreverent, story-driven, highly specific, quietly
  persuasive, like a business owner explaining something over lunch. [C1 → corpus]
- His teaching voice (jokes, tangents, occasional profanity) is much more
  colorful than his client-facing voice (calm, concise, buyer-focused). Copy
  the client-facing voice; never copy the profanity. [C1 → D18, D19]
- Openings like "Hey," "So," "By the way," "I want to show you…"; lots of
  contractions; small, permission-based CTAs. [C1 → corpus]
- Teaches by contrast: the bad way (the "Hustle Bro" character) versus a
  simpler alternative. [C1 → public videos]
- Everyday analogies: shoe stores, finger pointers, "the undefined more,"
  old plan vs. new plan. [C1 → corpus]
- The goal is for the buyer to understand, not to admire the writer. [C1]
