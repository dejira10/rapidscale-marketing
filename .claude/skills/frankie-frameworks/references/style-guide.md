# Frankie Fihn-Informed Tone, Voice & Communication Style Guide

Source: **G1**, supplied by the user on 2026-10-29. It summarizes the
communication principles from Frankie's training. The examples in it
(electrician, Nashville, roofing follow-up, generator estimates) are
**illustrative**; they aren't quotes from Frankie or real client results. For
the transcript evidence behind each principle, see `voice.md` and the topic
files.

**This guide is the rulebook for every Frankie-style draft.** Where it's
stricter than a transcript, for example on profanity, humour in cold outreach,
or impersonation, this guide wins.

## 1. Core Voice

Write like an experienced, down-to-earth business owner explaining a useful
idea to another business owner over coffee.

The voice should feel:

- Conversational, relaxed and natural.
- Direct, confident and knowledgeable without arrogance.
- Friendly, approachable and occasionally humorous.
- Honest, practical and grounded in real-world situations.
- Slightly contrarian when challenging conventional marketing advice.
- Focused on helping people rather than impressing them.
- Clear enough for someone with no marketing background to understand.

Never use profanity. Keep the personality, humour, confidence and
straightforward delivery without offensive language.

Do not impersonate Frankie or claim to be speaking on his behalf. Apply the
communication principles demonstrated in his training.

## 2. How to Speak to Business Owners

Talk to business owners like regular people, not marketing executives. Use
contractions, plain English and everyday language.

Expressions that fit the voice:

- "Here's what typically happens..."
- "Think about it this way..."
- "Let me give you an example."
- "Here's what I would do."
- "Now, here's where it gets interesting."
- "The problem isn't necessarily..."
- "What if you could..."
- "By the way..."
- "Does that make sense?"
- "Want me to show you what I mean?"

Use them only where they fit naturally, never mechanically. Avoid corporate
jargon, complicated explanations and inflated marketing language.

## 3. Describe Problems at Street Level

Never stop at vague statements such as:

- "You need more customers."
- "Your business needs better marketing."
- "You're wasting time."
- "You need higher-quality leads."

Describe exactly how the problem shows up during an ordinary workday.

- **Weak:** "Electrical contractors struggle with inconsistent leads."
- **Better:** "You've got three electricians on payroll, two trucks sitting in
  the parking lot, and nothing booked for Thursday. But payroll is still due
  Friday."

This is the Finger Pointer principle.

1. Walk through the owner's existing process.
2. Find the specific moment it becomes frustrating, expensive, embarrassing or
   exhausting.
3. Make it so recognizable that the reader thinks, "That's exactly what happens
   to us."

Use vivid, concrete situations, but don't invent actual customer experiences.

## 4. Use the Customer's Language

Good marketing language comes from listening to customers. Research:

- What they're frustrated about.
- What they're trying to accomplish.
- What they've already tried.
- What they dislike about their current situation.
- What they wish they had more of.
- What they're afraid will happen.
- Which jobs or customers they enjoy most.

Use verified customer wording whenever possible. If you create hypothetical
owner statements, label them as illustrative rather than presenting them as
real quotes.

Speak in terms of jobs, crews, trucks, customers, estimates, appointments,
payroll, money and time. Avoid terminology the buyer wouldn't naturally use.

## 5. Focus on Specific Outcomes

Don't sell vague promises such as "more leads, more sales, and more customers."
Identify one desirable result. Examples:

- One qualified generator installation estimate.
- One recovered mold remediation opportunity.
- One introduction to a builder with a relevant upcoming project.
- One previous customer who's ready to discuss a new appointment.

A specific result should be meaningful, believable and measurable. Never
guarantee outcomes without adequate evidence or control over delivery.

## 6. Explain the Old Plan Versus the New Plan

Show what the buyer currently does to get the result, then explain a clearly
different, desirable alternative.

- **Old plan:** spend evenings calling homeowners who never responded to their
  roofing estimates.
- **New plan:** use a structured follow-up process to identify which homeowners
  still want the work, then hand interested prospects back to the contractor.

Explain the practical difference. Don't invent complicated proprietary
terminology to make an ordinary service sound revolutionary.

## 7. Apply TIMER

Consider five motivations and concerns:

- **Time:** how much time does this save or require?
- **Identity:** does it help the buyer spend more time doing the work they want
  to do?
- **Money:** what's the economic value, cost or risk?
- **Energy:** how much effort does the buyer need to contribute?
- **Reputation:** could the approach improve or harm how the buyer is perceived?

Don't assume every owner wants more revenue above all else. Some want fewer
responsibilities, better customers, more freedom or less chaos.

## 8. Keep Cold Outreach Short

The first message starts a conversation. It isn't a complete sales
presentation. It has three elements:

1. A genuine, specific observation about the recipient.
2. One relevant problem, outcome or useful idea.
3. A casual, permission-based question.

Example:

> Hey, noticed you handle both residential and commercial electrical work
> around Nashville.
>
> Had an idea for getting more panel-upgrade estimates without chasing another
> bunch of small repair calls.
>
> Mind if I share it?

Never invent personal observations or pretend to have audited a business. Don't
include long company introductions, service lists, exaggerated promises or
pressure to book a call. Make it easy to reply in a few words.

## 9. Write Handraisers Like Invitations

A handraiser tests interest. It should feel like inviting someone to hear an
interesting idea, not a hard sales pitch.

**Formula:** who it's for + recognizable problem + specific desirable outcome +
easy invitation.

Keep it short. The aim is a genuine expression of interest, not an immediate
sale.

## 10. Ask Questions Naturally

When someone expresses interest, don't dump the whole offer on them. Start by
understanding their situation:

- Ask one useful question at a time.
- Move from general statements to specific experiences.

If someone says "We're not getting enough good jobs," explore:

- What kinds of jobs are coming in?
- Which jobs would they prefer?
- What's happening with the estimates?
- What have they tried?
- What would a good result look like?

Let their answers guide the next question. Don't follow a rigid script when the
conversation already gives you the answer.

## 11. Present the Plan Before the Pitch

1. Once you understand the situation, explain what you'd recommend in three to
   five simple steps. Make each step concrete.
2. Ask what the prospect thinks of the plan.
3. If they disagree, investigate the concern before moving to pricing.
4. If the plan makes sense to them, explain what implementation would involve.

The goal is mutual understanding, not pressure.

## 12. Write Google Docs and Sales Presentations Simply

Use this progression:

1. Demonstrate the problem or desired result.
2. Explain who the solution is suitable for.
3. Show the three-to-five-step implementation plan.
4. Present the investment, value and qualifications.
5. Explain exactly how to proceed, ask a question or decline.

Prefer one tangible example over dozens of abstract performance metrics. Be
transparent about limitations, responsibilities and what isn't included.

## 13. Follow Up With Additional Value

Avoid repeatedly asking:

- "Did you see my email?"
- "Any updates?"
- "Are you ready to move forward?"

Instead, expand on a useful part of the plan you already discussed. Example:

> I took another look at the kinds of electrical jobs you're trying to attract.
> One thing I'd focus on is separating panel-upgrade inquiries from general
> repair calls, so your team knows which appointments are worth prioritizing.

Only describe research or work that was actually done. Respect declines and
opt-outs.

## 14. Use Humor, Analogies, and Personality

Use simple comparisons that make complex ideas easy to understand:

- Shoe buyers versus people who never buy shoes.
- Shoe stores as places where buyers already gather.
- Coffee date, first date and marriage as rising levels of commitment.
- A restaurant selling the meal instead of describing its kitchen equipment.
- Showing someone the finished deck instead of advertising the hammer.

Humour should feel spontaneous and appropriate. Be entertaining when teaching;
be more restrained in cold outreach and client-facing proposals.

## 15. Things to Avoid

Never use:

- Fake urgency or manufactured scarcity.
- Exaggerated claims.
- Generic compliments.
- Invented testimonials or results.
- Unnecessary technical jargon.
- Aggressive closing tactics.
- Excessive adjectives or buzzwords.
- Long explanations where a short example works.
- Robotic question sequences.
- Profanity.

Avoid phrases such as "revolutionary solution", "skyrocket your business" and
"unlock unprecedented growth".

Don't lead with AI, automations, SEO, software or other technical tools unless
the buyer specifically cares about them. Lead with the result the buyer wants.

## 16. The Six Ps of Pre-Selling

Across longer-form sales content, address:

1. **Problem:** what specific situation is the buyer dealing with?
2. **Plan:** what's the different, desirable way forward?
3. **Price:** what does implementation cost, and how does that compare with its
   value?
4. **Philosophy:** what do we believe about solving this problem, and why?
5. **Personality:** what makes the communication genuine and human?
6. **Proof:** what credible demonstrations, evidence or results support the
   offer?

Not every short message needs all six. Use them across the right stages of the
buying experience.

## 17. Final Quality Check

Before delivering any marketing material, ask:

- Does this sound like a real person talking?
- Would the intended buyer recognize the situation?
- Am I describing a real-world symptom instead of a vague problem?
- Does the message focus on the buyer rather than us?
- Is the desired outcome specific and believable?
- Is the proposed plan easy to understand?
- Have I avoided unsupported claims?
- Is the next step small and comfortable?
- Could this be said naturally in a conversation?

**The guiding principle:** be clear before clever. Be specific before
persuasive. Make the offer desirable enough that the buyer doesn't need to be
pressured into buying it.
